Lay summary

Are bicycle injuries, and especially collisions between cars and bicycles, a public-health matter? This review looked at 824 studies and official statistics to place them among other causes of death and injury. The numbers are small beside the major causes. In Canada about 71 cyclists die each year, about one death in 4,200, and about 4,300 are admitted to hospital, one or two of every hundred hospital stays for injury. Falls, poisonings and suicide each cause many times more deaths. A car or truck is involved in about three in five of the deaths, but in only about one in seven to one in four of the hospital stays: most cyclists in hospital fell or hit something else. Police statistics count only about a tenth of the cyclists treated in hospital. When a cyclist dies, a head injury is usually involved. Helmets clearly protect the head in a crash. Canadian studies, two in Montréal, found fewer injuries on cycle paths separated from traffic and where cars move slowly, on few sites. The strictest review found too few rigorous studies to conclude; a broader 2026 review found separated paths the most consistently beneficial. Injuries are more common in poorer neighbourhoods. Cycling is good for health, and studies agree its benefits outweigh the risk of injury. Québec's and Montréal's public-health agencies already call road safety a public-health issue and support safe cycling infrastructure. The review concludes that cyclists' safety is a legitimate public-health concern, unequal and partly preventable, but not among the largest health burdens, and that no study has measured how many injuries Montréal's current network causes or could prevent. Whether that makes it a priority is for public-health authorities to judge. The review makes no recommendation. It was prepared with an AI system and has not been peer reviewed.
